Reference is made to the March Announcement in relation to the Company entering into
the Letter of Intent with the Seller in relation to the Acquisition. The Board is pleased to
announce that, on 7 April 2026, the Purchaser, a wholly-owned subsidiary of the Company,
entered into the Share Purchase Agreement with the Seller and the Target Company, pursuant
LETTER FROM THE BOARD

to which (i) the Purchaser conditionally agreed to acquire, and the Seller conditionally agreed

mainly including Marketing Authorization Holder (MAH) rights, trademark and broad
commercial rights for 14 branded chronic disease products across multiple countries and
regions in the Asia Pacific region. For the financial year ended 31 December 2025, the Target
Company recorded a normalized revenue of US$82,232,000 (approximately RMB577,992,000)
with an EBITDA of US$27,546,000 (approximately RMB193,615,000).
The purpose of this circular is to provide you with (i) further details of the Acquisition;
(ii) financial information on the Group; (iii) the Accountants’ Report on the financial
information of the Target Company; and (iv) other information as required under the Listing
Rules.
2. THE SHARE PURCHASE AGREEMENT
Date
7 April 2026
Parties
(i) the Seller;
(ii) the Purchaser; and
(iii) the Target Company.
Subject matter
Subject to the terms and conditions of the Share Purchase Agreement, at Closing, the
Seller shall (i) sell to the Purchaser the Sale Share, and the Purchaser shall purchase such
Sale Share from the Seller, together with all rights, dividends, entitlements, advantages
attaching thereto on and from the Closing Date; and (ii) assign to the Purchaser, and the
Purchaser shall succeed, all its existing rights and obligations in relation of the
Shareholder Loan. The Sale Share shall be sold free and clear of any encumbrances
except for the Agreed Pledge.
The Agreed Pledge was entered into in relation to a loan granted by the Bank to
HASTEN BIOPHARMECEUTICALS LOANCO LIMITED, the sole shareholder of the
Seller, prior and unconnected to the Acquisition and did not involve the Company. The
release of the Agreed Pledge is a condition precedent to the Acquisition and as at the
Latest Practicable Date, the Company understood that the Seller and the Bank were in
process of releasing the Agreed Pledge to facilitate the transfer of title over the Sale
Share from the Seller to the Purchaser.

In addition, subject to the terms and conditions of the Share Purchase Agreement, at
Closing, the Seller agrees to assign to the Purchaser, and the Purchaser agrees to succeed,
all its existing rights and obligations in relation of the Shareholder Loan (being the
rights and obligations as a lender, including the right to receive the repayment of principal
amount and accrued interest). As at the Latest Practicable Date, the outstanding
principal amount of the Shareholder Loan was US$148,804,000 (approximately
RMB1,045,914,000). Based on the currently applicable terms, the Shareholder Loan is
repayable upon demand and the interest rate is 5% per annum. For the avoidance of
doubt, the consideration for such Shareholder Loan is fully included in the consideration
and no additional payments shall be incurred.
Consideration and payment terms
The aggregate consideration payable by the Purchaser to the Seller under the Share
Purchase Agreement is US$250,000,000 (approximately RMB1,757,200,000), payable in
three installments as follows:
(i) US$150,000,000 (approximately RMB1,054,320,000), representing 60% of the
consideration, payable at Closing, subject to the satisfaction or waiver by the
Purchaser of the conditions precedent to Closing;
(ii) US$50,000,000 (approximately RMB351,440,000), representing 20% of the
consideration (the ‘‘Second Installment’’), which is expected to become due
and payable during the first quarter of 2028 (i.e. by no later than 31 March
2028), subject to the satisfaction or waiver by the Purchaser of the conditions
precedent to the payment of the Second Installment; and
(iii) USD50,000,000 (approximately RMB351,440,000), representing 20% of the
consideration (the ‘‘Third Installment’’), which is expected to become due and
payable during the first quarter of 2029 (i.e. by no later than 31 March 2029),
subject to the satisfaction or waiver by the Purchaser of the conditions
precedent to the payment of the Third Installment.
The Board considers the deferred payment arrangement to be fair and reasonable and

Valuation Methodology
The value analysis methods generally fall into three basic categories: the asset-based
approach, the market approach, and the income approach. The following provides a brief
introduction to these three value analysis methods:
(i) The asset-based approach refers to a general term for various valuation methods
that first calculate and analyze the replacement cost of assets, then assess and
quantify various depreciation factors already affecting those assets and finally
deduct these depreciation factors from the replacement cost to arrive at the
asset’s value. The fundamental premise of the asset-based approach is to
reconstruct or replace the asset itself. This analytical technique assumes that
any potential investor, when deciding whether to invest in an asset, will not be
willing to pay more than the current replacement cost of building that asset. If
the investment object is not brand new, the price the investor is willing to pay
will be the replacement cost of a brand-new asset minus various depreciation
factors. The asset-based approach is a widely used valuation methodology.
(ii) The market approach refers to a general term for various methods that use the
recent transaction prices of similar or comparable assets in the market and,
through direct comparison or analogous analysis, calculate the value of an asset.
The market approach is an appraisal technique that, based on the principle of
substitution, employs comparative and analogical reasoning and methodologies
to determine asset value. This analytical approach holds that any rational
LETTER FROM THE BOARD
– 16 –

[PDF PAGE 19]
investor, when acquiring an asset, would not be willing to pay more than the
current market price of a substitute asset with the same intended use. The
market approach is the most straightforward and persuasive valuation method.
(iii) The income approach refers to a general term for various analytical methods
that assess asset value by analyzing the present value of an asset’s expected
future earnings. The income approach employs capitalization and discounting
techniques and methodologies to evaluate and analyze asset value. This
valuation technique is based on the premise that any rational investor, when
acquiring or investing in an asset, will not be willing to pay or invest more than
the amount of money that the acquired or invested asset can generate in the
future, in other words, its expected returns. By utilizing technical tools such as
investment return analysis and discounted cash flow calculations, the income
approach takes the asset’s anticipated output capacity and profitability as the
key factors for analyzing and determining its value.
Given the characteristics of the Target Company, there are substantial limitations to
both the income approach and the cost approach in valuing the Target Company. In
particular:
(i) the income approach requires subjective assumptions to which the valuation is
highly sensitive, and even small changes in key inputs (such as discount rates,
growth assumptions, or project timing) may lead to significant fluctuations in
the valuation outcome;
(ii) the cost approach is based on the replacement or reproduction cost of the
underlying assets, which does not adequately capture the value of the Target
Company as going concern; and
(iii) the Target Company derives significant value from intangible factors such as
established operations as well as future earning potential, which are not fully
reflected under the cost approach.
In view of the above, the Valuer adopted the market approach for the valuation of
the Target Company. The market approach is applied to companies for which sufficient
and reliable comparable publicly listed companies can be identified, allowing market￾derived indicators to serve as appropriate basis for estimating value.
Given the characteristics of the Target Company, EV/EBITDA was adopted as the
primary valuation multiple. EV/EBITDA is widely used in valuing pharmaceutical
companies as it reflects the operating performance of the business before the impact of
capital structure, taxation and non-cash accounting items. In addition, EBITDA serves as
a proxy for the underlying cash generating ability of the Target Company. Compared with
net profit-based multiples, EV/EBITDA is less affected by differences in financing
arrangements, depreciation policies and tax positions across comparable companies,
thereby enhancing comparability.

Valuation Methodology
We have reviewed the Valuation Report and discussed with the Valuer
methodologies of, and bases and assumptions adopted for the Valuation, and
adjustments made to arrive at the Valuation. As advised by the Valuer, in arriving at
the fair value of 100% equity interest in the Target Company, the Valuer has
considered three generally accepted approaches, namely asset-based approach,
market approach and income approach.
(i) The asset-based approach refers to a general term for various valuation
methods that first calculate and analyze the replacement cost of assets,
then assess and quantify various depreciation factors already affecting
those assets and finally deduct these depreciation factors from the
replacement cost to arrive at the asset’s value. The fundamental premise of
the asset-based approach is to reconstruct or replace the asset itself. This
analytical technique assumes that any potential investor, when deciding
whether to invest in an asset, will not be willing to pay more than the
current replacement cost of building that asset. If the investment object is
not brand new, the price the investor is willing to pay will be the
replacement cost of a brand-new asset minus various depreciation factors.
The asset-based approach is a widely used valuation methodology.
(ii) The market approach refers to a general term for various methods that use
the recent transaction prices of similar or comparable assets in the market
and, through direct comparison or analogous analysis, calculate the value
of an asset. The market approach is an appraisal technique that, based on
the principle of substitution, employs comparative and analogical
reasoning and methodologies to determine asset value. This analytical
approach holds that any rational investor, when acquiring an asset, would
not be willing to pay more than the current market price of a substitute
asset with the same intended use. The market approach is the most
straightforward and persuasive valuation method.
(iii) The income approach refers to a general term for various analytical
methods that assess asset value by analyzing the present value of an asset’s
expected future earnings. The income approach employs capitalization and
discounting techniques and methodologies to evaluate and analyze asset
value. This valuation technique is based on the premise that any rational
investor, when acquiring or investing in an asset, will not be willing to pay
or invest more than the amount of money that the acquired or invested
asset can generate in the future, in other words, its expected returns. By
utilizing technical tools such as investment return analysis and discounted
cash flow calculations, the income approach takes the asset’s anticipated
output capacity and profitability as the key factors for analyzing and
determining its value.

Given the characteristics of the Target Company, there are substantial
limitations to both the income approach and the asset-based approach for valuing the
Target Company. Firstly, the income approach requires subjective assumptions to
which the valuation is highly sensitive, and even small changes in key inputs (such
as discount rates, growth assumptions, or project timing) may lead to significant
fluctuations in the valuation outcome. Secondly, the asset-based approach is based
on the replacement or reproduction cost of the underlying assets, which does not
adequately capture the value of the Target Company as going concern and the Target
Company derives significant value from intangible factors such as established
operations, market position and company relationships, as well as future earning
potential, which are not fully reflected under the asset-based approach. In view of
the above, we concur with the Valuer in adopting the market approach for the
Valuation of the Target Company, as a sufficient number of reliable comparable
publicly listed companies can be identified, allowing market-derived indicators to
serve as appropriate basis for estimating value.

Discount for Lack of Marketability (‘‘DLOM’’)
As set out in the Valuation Report, liquidity refers to the marketability of equity
interests, that is, the speed and ease with which owners can convert their equity
interests into cash when they choose to sell them. The application of a discount for
lack of marketability reflects the fact that equity interests in private companies do
not have an established trading market. As a result, equity interests in private
companies are generally less readily marketable than those of publicly listed
companies. As the Target Company is not listed, the Valuer has applied a DLOM of
20.1%, with reference to the average DLOM figures from the Control Premium &
Discount for Lack of Marketability Study (February 2026) published by Moore Hong
Kong. Following our independent review, we note from the study that Moore Hong
Kong reviewed a total of 331 notifiable transaction circulars issued by listed
companies across various industries and published on the Stock Exchange website
for the trailing twelve months ended 31 December 2025. Of these 331 transactions,
only 33 included valuation reports with the adoption of DLOM adjustments. The
average DLOM of 20.1% was calculated by Moore Hong Kong based on this full set
of 33 relevant transactions, and we consider the sample size sufficient.

the holder of a controlling interest has the ability to influence the company’s cash

typically carries a higher value than a minority interest, which is subject to the
decisions, governance practices and management capabilities of the controlling
shareholders. The Valuer has adopted a control premium of 23.9%, with reference to
the average control premium figures from the Control Premium & Discount for Lack

2.3 MATERIAL ACCOUNTING POLICY INFORMATION
Impairment of non-financial assets
Where an indication of impairment exists, or when annual impairment testing for an asset is required
(other than inventories and financial assets), the asset’s recoverable amount is estimated. An asset’s
recoverable amount is the higher of the asset’s or cash-generating unit’s value in use and its fair value less
costs of disposal, and is determined for an individual asset, unless the asset does not generate cash inflows
that are largely independent of those from other assets or groups of assets, in which case the recoverable
amount is determined for the cash-generating unit to which the asset belongs.
An impairment loss is recognised only if the carrying amount of an asset exceeds its recoverable
amount. In assessing value in use, the estimated future cash flows are discounted to their present value using
a pre-tax discount rate that reflects current market assessments of the time value of money and the risks
specific to the asset. An impairment loss is charged to profit or loss in the period in which it arises in those
expense categories consistent with the function of the impaired asset.
An assessment is made at the end of each reporting period as to whether there is an indication that
previously recognised impairment losses may no longer exist or may have decreased. If such an indication
exists, the recoverable amount is estimated. A previously recognised impairment loss of an asset other than
goodwill is reversed only if there has been a change in the estimates used to determine the recoverable
amount of that asset, but not to an amount higher than the carrying amount that would have been determined
(net of any depreciation/amortisation) had no impairment loss been recognised for the asset in prior years. A
reversal of such an impairment loss is credited to the profit or loss in the period in which it arises.

Impairment of financial assets
The Target Company recognises an allowance for expected credit losses (‘‘ECLs’’) for all debt
instruments. ECLs are based on the difference between the contractual cash flows due in accordance with the
contract and all the cash flows that the Target Company expects to receive, discounted at an approximation of
the original effective interest rate. The expected cash flows will include cash flows from the sale of collateral
held or other credit enhancements that are integral to the contractual terms.
General approach
ECLs are recognised in two stages. For credit exposures for which there has not been a significant
increase in credit risk since initial recognition, ECLs are provided for credit losses that result from default
events that are possible within the next 12 months (a 12-month ECL). For those credit exposures for which
there has been a significant increase in credit risk since initial recognition, a loss allowance is required for
credit losses expected over the remaining life of the exposure, irrespective of the timing of the default (a
lifetime ECL).
At each reporting date, the Target Company assesses whether the credit risk on a financial instrument
has increased significantly since initial recognition. When making the assessment, the Target Company
compares the risk of a default occurring on the financial instrument as at the reporting date with the risk of a
default occurring on the financial instrument as at the date of initial recognition and considers reasonable and
supportable information that is available without undue cost or effort, including historical and forward￾looking information. The Target Company considers that there has been a significant increase in credit risk
when contractual payments are more than 30 days past due.
The Target Company considers a financial asset in default when contractual payments are 90 days past
due. However, in certain cases, the Target Company may also consider a financial asset to be in default when
internal or external information indicates that the Target Company is unlikely to receive the outstanding
contractual amounts in full before taking into account any credit enhancements held by the Target Company.
A financial asset is written off when there is no reasonable expectation of recovering the contractual
cash flows.

Stage 1 — Financial instruments for which credit risk has not increased significantly since initial
recognition and for which the loss allowance is measured at an amount equal to 12-

4. VALUE ANALYSIS METHODS
4.1 General Value Analysis Method
The value analysis methods generally fall into three basic categories: the asset-based
approach, the market approach, and the income approach. The following provides a brief
introduction to these three value analysis methods:
The asset-based approach refers to a general term for various valuation methods that
first calculate and analyze the replacement cost of assets, then assess and quantify various
depreciation factors already affecting those assets and finally deduct these depreciation
factors from the replacement cost to arrive at the asset’s value. The fundamental premise
of the asset-based approach is to reconstruct or replace the asset itself. This analytical
technique assumes that any potential investor, when deciding whether to invest in an
asset, will not be willing to pay more than the current replacement cost of building that
asset. If the investment object is not brand new, the price the investor is willing to pay
will be the replacement cost of a brand-new asset minus various depreciation factors. The
asset-based approach is a widely used valuation methodology. The asset-based approach
refers to a general term for various valuation methods that first calculate and analyze the
replacement cost of assets, then assess and quantify various depreciation factors already
affecting those assets and finally deduct these depreciation factors from the replacement
cost to arrive at the asset’s value. The fundamental premise of the asset-based approach is
to reconstruct or replace the asset itself. This analytical technique assumes that any
potential investor, when deciding whether to invest in an asset, will not be willing to pay
more than the current replacement cost of building that asset. If the investment object is
not brand-new, the price the investor is willing to pay will be the replacement cost of a
brand-new asset minus various depreciation factors. The asset-based approach is a widely
used valuation methodology.

The market approach refers to a general term for various methods that use the recent
transaction prices of similar or comparable assets in the market and, through direct
comparison or analogous analysis, calculate the value of an asset. The market approach is
an appraisal technique that, based on the principle of substitution, employs comparative
and analogical reasoning and methodologies to determine asset value. This analytical
approach holds that any rational investor, when acquiring an asset, would not be willing
to pay more than the current market price of a substitute asset with the same intended use.
The market approach is the most straightforward and persuasive valuation method.
The income approach refers to a general term for various analytical methods that
assess asset value by analyzing the present value of an asset’s expected future earnings.
The income approach employs capitalization and discounting techniques and
methodologies to evaluate and analyze asset value. This valuation technique is based on
the premise that any rational investor, when acquiring or investing in an asset, will not be
willing to pay or invest more than the amount of money that the acquired or invested
asset can generate in the future, in other words, its expected returns. By utilizing technical
tools such as investment return analysis and discounted cash flow calculations, the income
approach takes the asset’s anticipated output capacity and profitability as the key factors
for analyzing and determining its value.
Given the characteristics of the Target Company, there are substantial limitations to
both the income approach and the cost approach in valuing the Target Company. In
particular:
(i) the income approach requires subjective assumptions to which the valuation is
highly sensitive, and even small changes in key inputs (such as discount rates,
growth assumptions, or project timing) may lead to significant fluctuations in
the valuation outcome;
(ii) the cost approach is based on the replacement or reproduction cost of the
underlying assets, which does not adequately capture the value of the Target
Company as going concerns; and
(iii) the Target Company derive significant value from intangible factors such as
established operations, market position and company relationships, as well as
their future earning potential, which are not fully reflected under the cost
approach.
In view of the above, we have adopted the market approach for the valuation of the
Target Company. The market approach is applied to company for which sufficient and
reliable comparable publicly listed company can be identified, allowing market-derived
indicators to serve as appropriate basis for estimating value.

4.3 Other Valuation Considerations
4.3.1 Discount for Lack of Marketability (‘‘DLOM’’)
Liquidity refers to the marketability of equity interests, that is, the speed and
ease with which owners can convert their equity interests into cash when they choose
to sell them. The application of a discount for lack of marketability reflects the fact
APPENDIX V VALUATION REPORT
– V-11 –

[PDF PAGE 114]
that equity interests in private companies do not have an established trading market.
As a result, equity interests in private companies are generally less readily
marketable than those of publicly listed companies. Accordingly, the value of equity
interests in a private company is typically lower than that of comparable equity
interests in publicly listed companies.
In this valuation analysis, where the market approach using the market multiple
method is applied, the discount for lack of marketability of 20.1% is adopted, with
reference to the average DLOM figures from the Moore Control Premium &
Discount for Lack of Marketability Study issued in February 2026 representing the
average statistics reported in the study for the trailing 12-month period ended 31
December 2025.
4.3.2 Control Premium (‘‘CP’’)
A controlling interest in a company provides significant advantages in decision￾making with respect to business operations and strategic development. In particular,
the holder of a controlling interest has the ability to influence the company’s cash
flow allocation and discretionary expenditures. As a result, a controlling interest
typically carries a higher value than a minority interest, which is subject to the
decisions, governance practices and management capabilities of the controlling
shareholders.
In this valuation analysis, where the market approach using the market multiple
method is applied, the control premium of 23.9% is adopted, with reference to the
average CP figures from the Moore Control Premium & Discount for Lack of
Marketability Study issued in February 2026 representing the average statistics
reported in the study for the trailing 12-month period ended 31 December 2025.
100% Equity Value (before shareholder loan) on non-marketable and controlling
basis = Equity Value (pre adjustment)*(1–DLOM)*(1+CP).
